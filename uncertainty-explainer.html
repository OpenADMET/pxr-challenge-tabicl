Uncertain uncertainties

Plotting |error| against model uncertainty and judging by Spearman rank correlation might not be the right framing. Here we separate the concepts and re-examine the evidence.

00 Calibration vs. ranking

Uncertainty estimates can answer two distinct questions. They sound similar but test different properties and can give opposite verdicts on the same model.

Calibration

Are the error bars the right width? If the model says "90% interval," do 90% of true values actually land inside it? This is a property of the population. You pool all 260 compounds and ask one question with 260 data points answering it.

Ranking / discrimination

Can the model tell you which compounds are hard? Do the ones with wider predicted intervals actually produce larger errors? This is a property of individual compounds. You need each compound's predicted uncertainty to line up with its realized error.

Calibration asks: on average, are the intervals honest? Ranking asks: for each compound, does the model know where it will be wrong? A model can pass one and fail the other.

Every equation and symbol on this page shows its definition on hover and has an entry in the equations and symbols reference (section 11). Clicking a symbol jumps to its entry.

02 The question from a commenter

Jonas Landsgesell pushed back with a toy example: a well-calibrated linear model whose Spearman ρ between predicted interval width and absolute error was essentially zero. His question:

The challenge

"I don't see exactly why you expect a strong correlation there. Did you investigate a data-generating process from which you would expect a different Spearman correlation?"

In plain terms: what if ρ = 0.26 is normal, even when the uncertainty estimates are perfectly calibrated? What if we declared the ranking bad without checking what "good" would look like?

03 Do the predicted uncertainties actually vary?

A low ρ has at least two explanations worth checking. The first is straightforward: the model might predict nearly the same σ for every compound, leaving no ranking to detect. If so, a low ρ would be expected regardless of how many measurements we had. Before looking for subtler causes, we need to check whether the σ values spread out enough to encode a ranking in the first place.

04 The one-flip problem

The σ values vary and the intervals are calibrated, yet the Spearman ρ is only 0.26. One explanation is that the Spearman ρ test, computed on individual residuals, lacks the statistical power to detect the ranking when each compound contributes only one realized error. The following analogy illustrates why.

The coin analogy

Imagine each compound has a coin. Compound A's coin lands heads 60% of the time (high σ, expect large errors). Compound B's coin lands heads 40% of the time (low σ, expect small errors).

If you flip each coin a thousand times, you can easily tell which coin has the higher rate. Compound A clearly produces more heads.

But you only flip each coin once. Compound A might land tails. Compound B might land heads. From those two flips, you'd rank them backwards.

The coin is the compound's residual, whether it comes from assay noise, model error, or a mix of both. Part of the error comes from the assay, and a repeat experiment flips that part again. Part comes from the model, and that part is flipped once, when the model is trained: the compound has one true value, and the model makes one prediction for it.

Our scatter plot has the same problem. Each compound's predicted σ defines a distribution of possible errors, and each residual is the distance between the prediction and one measurement. The chart below splits that distance into two parts: the gap between the prediction and the compound's true value (the model error, shown as the dashed orange bracket) and the gap between the true value and the measurement (the measurement noise, whose spread is the purple distribution). The true value is illustrative, a way to show where a residual comes from; the calculation of ρ uses only the measurement and the prediction (section 07).

A compound with a wide predicted distribution (high σ) will, on average, produce larger residuals, but on any single occasion it might land close to the predicted mean and show a small error. Wide distributions still produce values near their center.

So we're trying to correlate a property of a distribution (its width) with a property of a single sample from that distribution (how far that one sample landed from the center). With one sample per distribution, the rank correlation is weak, even when the uncertainty estimates are correct. That sample comes from the experiment and the prediction together: the residual is the gap between one measurement and one prediction, so assay noise and model error each contribute to it.

05 What does perfectly calibrated uncertainty produce?

How weak should ρ be? We ask what distribution of ρ values a model with ideal uncertainty estimates would produce from one realized error per compound, as in our data.

We take the 260 predicted σ values from the real model and pretend the uncertainty estimates are perfectly calibrated: for each compound, we draw a single residual from a Gaussian with that compound's σ, ei = |zi| · σi with z drawn from a standard normal. The point predictions can still be wrong (they are); what's "perfect" is only the error bars. We compute Spearman ρnull on those simulated residuals and repeat 2,000 times to build a distribution of ρnull values.

To be precise: "perfectly calibrated" here means each compound's true error really is drawn from the Gaussian the model prescribes. The model is not making perfect predictions (zero error). It is making imperfect predictions and correctly estimating how imperfect each one is. If our observed ρ = 0.26 falls inside that distribution, the low correlation reflects having one realized error per compound rather than a deficiency in the model's uncertainty. If it falls below, there's a real ranking problem that single draws cannot explain.

The result

Perfectly calibrated uncertainty with our σ distribution produces a mean Spearman ρnull of about 0.285. Our observed 0.26 sits at the 34th percentile of the null distribution, well within the 95% interval of [0.17, 0.40]. The ρ we observe is what we would expect even if the uncertainty estimates were ideal. The low correlation reflects the inherent noise of comparing a distribution's width to a single sample from it.

The blog post's conclusion, "not a reliable ranking of which compounds are hard," is too strong. The σ values vary, calibration holds, and higher σ does correspond to higher expected error, so the ranking is present. Any test based on individual residuals will show a weak correlation, because each residual is one noisy draw from the distribution it's being compared against. That would remain true with perfect measurements, because most of this model's error is model error rather than assay noise (section 06).

06 Would replicate measurements help?

Repeated measurements of each compound might seem to raise ρ. Replicates remove only the measurement part of the residual (yi − ŷi = (ti − ŷi) + (yi − ti)), so how much they help depends on how much of σ is measurement noise. If σ is calibrated, whatever part of it the measurement noise does not account for is model error. A toy simulation shows the range. Using our actual 260 predicted σ values, we simulate a perfectly calibrated model in which a fixed share of each compound's σ² is measurement noise and the rest is model error, measure each compound R times, and average the absolute residuals over replicates (mi = (1/R) · Σk eik). We also run two realistic noise levels: the standard error of each dose-response fit, si, which the challenge data reports (median 0.12 log units against a mean σ of 0.54, about 11% of σ² on average), and a constant 0.3 log units (about 28%).

Every curve starts at ρ ≈ 0.28 with one measurement, whatever the source of the error. With no measurement noise the curve stays flat, because replicates have nothing to average: each measurement is already the true value, and ρ still averages 0.28. Only the share that is measurement noise lets ρ rise, to 0.48 at 25% and 0.95 at 100% with 100 replicates. With the curve-fit si, ρ levels off near 0.36, and with a constant 0.3, near 0.42. The two realistic noise levels are not proportional to σ, so they do not line up exactly with the fixed-share curves of the same average share.

Verdict

The one-flip problem is therefore more than a shortage of measurements. For this model, most of it would remain with perfect data, because a correct σ describes the spread of a compound's possible errors, not the error it actually made. A high per-compound ρ would need σ values that differ by large factors between compounds. The ranking still shows when errors are averaged across compounds with similar σ, which does for the model-error draw what replicates do for the measurement draw; the quintile bins in section 09a work this way.

07 Would treating the measurements as true fix the ranking?

Section 04 explains the one-flip problem with a true value for each compound that differs from its measurement. That framing invites an objection: if the gap between the true value and the measurement causes the weak ranking, treating each measurement as the true value should remove it.

The calculation of ρ already does this. Spearman ρ compares σi with |yi − ŷi|, the distance from the prediction to the measurement, and no "true" value enters the calculation. Each measurement is the prediction target, so the observed ρ = 0.26 is the result of treating every measurement as the true value.

Treating the measurements as the true values does not change ρ, because ρ is already computed that way. The weak per-compound ranking comes from having one residual per compound, and it would be the same if every measurement equaled its true value.

08 Can measurement error improve the ranking?

Each pEC50 comes with the standard error of its dose-response fit, si (section 06). If σi describes the model's error and si the measurement's noise, the two normal distributions convolve, and a measurement should land around the prediction with variance σi² + si² (yi ~ N(ŷi, σi² + si²)). We score that combined spread against the measurements, next to σi and σi rescaled by a constant to the right average size.

Restricted to the measurable compounds, ρ for σ is 0.135, inside the range a correct σ produces for them (0.10 to 0.35).

Verdict

The curve-fit standard error improves the scores only on censored measurements below the assay floor, and the model's σ already flags those compounds. On the measurable compounds, it adds nothing to the ranking.

09 What this means in practice

The calibration plot (Figure 6 right in the blog post) already told us the intervals are the right width. The σ distribution tells us the model differentiates between compounds. Together, those two facts mean the uncertainty estimates are useful.

What you can do with them: use the predicted intervals directly. If compound A has a 90% interval of [4.5, 6.5] and compound B has [5.0, 5.4], pick B when you need precision. Over a portfolio of decisions, calibrated intervals with real σ variation will outperform ignoring them.

What you can't do: look at one compound's error after the fact and judge whether the model "knew" it was hard. That's the one-flip problem. Repeated measurements would average away the measurement noise, and the model's error on that compound would remain one draw from the distribution σ describes. Across a batch, the ranking is still present.

Several evaluation tools may handle the ranking question better than Spearman ρ on individual residuals, though each comes with its own assumptions and limitations. The first two (quintile binning and CRPS) address compound-level enrichment directly. The third (NLL) is an aggregate model-comparison metric that does not support enrichment.

The one-flip problem limits every evaluation tool that follows. Each method works around it (by aggregating, by scoring the full distribution, by testing calibration globally), but none eliminates it. Averaging across compounds is the main way around it. Replicate measurements would also sharpen all of them: more observations per compound would reduce the noise in per-compound error estimates, tighten the quintile bins, smooth the CRPS and NLL quintile curves, and raise Spearman ρ along the replicate curves in section 06, up to a plateau that depends on how much of σ is measurement noise. The evaluation tools below are the best we can do with one measurement per compound.

09a Quintile binning

The simplest approach: sort the 260 compounds by predicted σ, split them into five equal groups (Q1 to Q5, ~52 each), and compute the mean absolute residual in each bin. Pooling 52 compounds per group averages out the single-draw noise that makes the raw scatter plot so cloudy. If the ranking is real, mean error should increase across the bins.

Quintile binning: mean |residual| by predicted σ group
Mean absolute residual increases from 0.27 in the lowest-σ group (Q1) to 0.65 in the highest (Q5). The ranking is real; pooling compounds per bin reveals the trend that individual residuals obscure.

Strengths: Easy to compute and interpret. Answers the ranking question directly, without modeling assumptions. Weaknesses: The number of bins is arbitrary (5 here, but 4 or 6 would give a slightly different picture). Discretizing throws away information about the shape within each bin, and the approach gives no single comparable score to rank one model's uncertainty against another's.

09b Continuous ranked probability score (CRPS)

CRPS evaluates the full predicted distribution against each observed value. It measures the area between the predicted cumulative distribution function (CDF) F and a step function at the observed value: CRPS = ∫ (Fi(u) − 𝟙[u ≥ yi])² du. A prediction that concentrates probability near the true value scores well; one that spreads probability away from it scores poorly. Lower is better.

CRPS is a proper scoring rule, meaning a model scores best by reporting its true beliefs. It jointly rewards calibration (intervals the right width) and sharpness (intervals as narrow as possible while remaining calibrated). A model that predicts wide intervals for every compound will be calibrated but not sharp; CRPS penalizes the unnecessary width.

Breaking CRPS down by predicted σ quintile, as in section 09a, shows a monotonic increase, confirming that the model assigns higher uncertainty to compounds that genuinely score worse.

Mean CRPS by predicted σ quintile
CRPS increases monotonically from Q1 (0.18) to Q5 (0.46). Higher predicted σ corresponds to worse distributional fit, as expected when the model's uncertainty estimates track real difficulty.

Strengths: Scores each compound's whole predicted distribution, N(ŷi, σi²), against its measurement, where an error metric such as RMSE scores only the point prediction ŷi. Proper scoring rule (a model minimizes CRPS by reporting its true predicted distribution, so there is no incentive to distort the estimate). Works per-observation without binning. Produces a single comparable number across models. Weaknesses: Less intuitive than "did the true value land in the interval?" Requires the full predicted CDF (or a closed-form assumption, as with the Gaussian here). Can be dominated by a few large-error outliers.

09c Negative log-likelihood (NLL)

NLL answers a different question from the first two tools. Where quintile binning and CRPS ask "can the model rank which compounds are hard?", NLL asks "how good is the model's probabilistic prediction in aggregate?" It is a model-comparison metric, not a compound-level triage tool.

Under a Gaussian prediction with mean ŷ and standard deviation σ, NLL = 0.5 × log(2πσi²) + (yi − ŷi)² / (2σi²). The first term penalizes wide intervals (wasted probability); the second penalizes narrow intervals that miss the true value. Lower is better. NLL is a proper scoring rule, and compared to CRPS, it penalizes overconfidence more harshly: a single observation far outside a narrow interval produces a very large NLL, because the squared residual is divided by a small σ².

Unlike CRPS, NLL per predicted σ quintile is not monotonic: Q1 scores best (0.32), but Q2 and Q3 score worse than Q4. This happens because NLL penalizes both directions. A moderate-σ compound with one large residual (a bad single draw) gets hammered by the (yi − ŷi)² / σi² term, while a high-σ compound with the same residual absorbs it more gracefully. This non-monotonicity means NLL cannot be used for enrichment: sorting compounds by NLL would not reliably separate easy from hard predictions.

Mean NLL by predicted σ quintile
NLL is not monotonic across quintiles. Q1 benefits from narrow σ and small residuals. Q2 and Q3 are penalized by a few large residuals against moderate σ values. Q5 has the highest NLL because its residuals are genuinely large. The non-monotonicity reflects NLL's sensitivity to tail events, not a calibration failure.

Strengths: Proper scoring rule. The strictest aggregate test of whether σ matches the actual error distribution. Standard in probabilistic modeling; directly optimized by many training procedures. Useful for comparing one model's uncertainty against another's. Weaknesses: Does not support enrichment or compound-level triage (non-monotonic per quintile). Heavily influenced by outliers (a single large residual against a small σ dominates the average). Assumes a distributional form (Gaussian here). Harder to interpret than CRPS or binning in absolute terms.
