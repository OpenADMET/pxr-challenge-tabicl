Uncertain uncertainties

Plotting |error| against model uncertainty and judging by Spearman rank correlation might not be the right framing. Here we separate the concepts and re-examine the evidence.

00 Calibration vs. ranking

Uncertainty estimates can answer two distinct questions. They sound similar but test different properties and can give opposite verdicts on the same model.

Calibration

Are the error bars the right width? If the model says "90% interval," do 90% of true values actually land inside it? This is a property of the population. You pool all 260 compounds and ask one question with 260 data points answering it.

Ranking / discrimination

Can the model tell you which compounds are hard? Do the ones with wider predicted intervals actually produce larger errors? This is a property of individual compounds. You need each compound's predicted uncertainty to line up with its realized error.

Calibration asks: on average, are the intervals honest? Ranking asks: for each compound, does the model know where it will be wrong? A model can pass one and fail the other.

02 The question from a commenter

Jonas Landsgesell pushed back with a toy example: a well-calibrated linear model whose Spearman ρ between predicted interval width and absolute error was essentially zero. His question:

The challenge

"I don't see exactly why you expect a strong correlation there. Did you investigate a data-generating process from which you would expect a different Spearman correlation?"

In plain terms: what if ρ = 0.26 is normal, even when the uncertainty estimates are perfectly calibrated? What if we declared the ranking bad without checking what "good" would look like?

03 Do the predicted uncertainties actually vary?

A low ρ has at least two explanations worth checking. The first is straightforward: the model might predict nearly the same σ for every compound, leaving no ranking to detect. If so, a low ρ would be expected regardless of how many measurements we had. Before looking for subtler causes, we need to check whether the σ values spread out enough to encode a ranking in the first place.

Because expected absolute error scales with σ under a calibrated model (E[ei] = σi · √(2/π)), these varying σ values encode a ranking. So the low ρ must have another cause.

04 The one-flip problem

The σ values vary and the intervals are calibrated, yet the Spearman ρ is only 0.26. One explanation is that the Spearman ρ test, computed on individual residuals, lacks the statistical power to detect the ranking when each compound contributes only one observation. The following analogy illustrates why.

The coin analogy

Imagine each compound has a coin. Compound A's coin lands heads 60% of the time (high σ, expect large errors). Compound B's coin lands heads 40% of the time (low σ, expect small errors).

If you flip each coin a thousand times, you can easily tell which coin has the higher rate. Compound A clearly produces more heads.

But you only flip each coin once. Compound A might land tails. Compound B might land heads. From those two flips, you'd rank them backwards.

Our scatter plot has the same problem. Each compound's predicted σ defines a distribution of possible errors, and each residual is the distance between the prediction and one measurement. The chart below splits that distance into two parts: the gap between the prediction and the compound's true value (the model error, shown as the dashed orange bracket) and the gap between the true value and the measurement (the measurement noise, whose spread is the purple distribution).

A compound with a wide predicted distribution (high σ) will, on average, produce larger residuals, but on any single occasion it might land close to the predicted mean and show a small error. Wide distributions still produce values near their center.

So we're trying to correlate a property of a distribution (its width) with a property of a single sample from that distribution (how far that one sample landed from the center). With one sample per distribution, the rank correlation is weak, even when the uncertainty estimates are correct.

Each residual is a single draw in two senses. The measurement is one draw from the purple distribution, and a repeat experiment would land somewhere else. The model error is one draw too, because σ describes how large the errors of compounds like this one tend to be, and this compound's true residual is one realization of that spread. Repeat experiments average away the measurement draw. They cannot remove the model-error draw, because the compound has one true value and the model makes one prediction for it.

A toy simulation shows how much replicates could help. Using our actual 260 predicted σ values, we simulate a perfectly calibrated model and ask what Spearman ρ we would observe if we measured each compound R times and averaged the absolute residuals over replicates (mi = (1/R) · Σk eik). Replicates average away only the measurement-noise part of the residual (yi − ŷi = (ti − ŷi) + (yi − ti)), so the answer depends on how much of σ is measurement noise. If all of σ were measurement noise, ρ would climb from 0.28 at R = 1 to 0.49 at R = 3 and 0.72 at R = 10. The challenge data reports the standard error of each dose-response fit, si, with a median of 0.12 log units against a mean σ of 0.54. If σ is calibrated, whatever part of it the measurement noise does not account for is model error. Treating si as the measurement noise leaves most of σ as model error, and ρ then rises only to 0.32 at R = 3 and levels off near 0.36. With a constant measurement noise of 0.3 log units, it levels off near 0.42.

This simulation draws residuals from the model's predicted σ values, which means it assumes those σ values are correct. That assumption is partially validated by the calibration plot (miscalibration area 0.04), but it is not an independent proof. The simulation demonstrates the mechanics of the one-flip problem (one draw per compound limits ρ even when the ranking is real) rather than proving that our model's specific σ values are right. It also assumes Gaussian errors, and the curves for the curve-fit standard error si and for a constant 0.3 log units treat that value as the full run-to-run variability. Real replicate measurements may vary more than that.

05 What does perfectly calibrated uncertainty produce?

The replicate curve in section 04 varied the number of measurements per compound to show how far replicates could raise ρ. This section uses the complementary approach: holding the replicate count at 1 (matching our data), it asks what distribution of ρ values a model with ideal uncertainty estimates would produce. Section 04 asked how many measurements it would take to see the ranking clearly; this section asks whether ρ = 0.26 indicates a ranking deficiency or is simply the expected outcome given one measurement per compound.

We take the 260 predicted σ values from the real model and pretend the uncertainty estimates are perfectly calibrated: for each compound, we draw a single residual from a Gaussian with that compound's σ, ei = |zi| · σi with z drawn from a standard normal. The point predictions can still be wrong (they are); what's "perfect" is only the error bars. We compute Spearman ρnull on those simulated residuals and repeat 2,000 times to build a distribution of ρnull values.

To be precise: "perfectly calibrated" here means each compound's true error really is drawn from the Gaussian the model prescribes. The model is not making perfect predictions (zero error). It is making imperfect predictions and correctly estimating how imperfect each one is. If our observed ρ = 0.26 falls inside that distribution, the low correlation reflects the measurement setup (one residual per compound) rather than a deficiency in the model's uncertainty. If it falls below, there's a real ranking problem the measurement noise cannot explain.

06 The result

Perfectly calibrated uncertainty with our σ distribution produces a mean Spearman ρnull of about 0.285. Our observed 0.26 sits at the 34th percentile of the null distribution, well within the 95% interval of [0.17, 0.40]. The ρ we observe is what we would expect even if the uncertainty estimates were ideal. The low correlation reflects the inherent noise of comparing a distribution's width to a single sample from it.

The blog post's conclusion, "not a reliable ranking of which compounds are hard," is too strong. The σ values vary, calibration holds, and higher σ does correspond to higher expected error, so the ranking is present. Any test based on individual residuals will show a weak correlation, because each residual is one noisy draw from the distribution it's being compared against.

07 Resampling each measurement with its curve-fit standard error

A natural follow-up is to ask whether error information we already have can help. Each pEC50 comes from a dose-response fit, and the challenge data reports the standard error of that fit, si, for every compound. The idea is to treat each measured value as the center of a normal distribution with width si, draw a new value for every compound (y*i = yi + si · zi), recompute ρ, and repeat, which gives a distribution of ρ that accounts for the uncertainty in each measurement. The method requires no replicates and no new experiments, so we tested whether it tells us more about the ranking than the single measurement does.

The idea rests on a common convention: the measured value is the center of its distribution, and the curve-fit standard error is the width. The convention can be read three ways, and each gives resampled ρ a different meaning.

  • The measurement is the true value plus noise. Resampling adds a second noise draw to the first, and the question is whether resampled ρ moves toward the ρ we would see against the true values, which we call the oracle ρ.
  • The measurement is the true value. The observed ρ of 0.26 is then already the oracle ρ, and the question is what resampling does to an exact quantity.
  • The measurement locates the true value to within its standard error. With one measurement and nothing else to go on, the true value is as likely to lie on one side of it as the other, so the plausible true values follow N(yi, si²), the posterior under a flat prior. Resampled values are then draws of plausible true values, and resampled ρ becomes an estimate of the oracle ρ.

A simulation in which the true values are known tests the first and third readings, and resampling the real measurements tests the second.

The curve-fit standard errors are small and correlate with σ

Curve-fit standard error of the 260 test pEC50 values
Histogram bins are 0.025 log units wide. Dashed lines mark the median standard error (0.12), a constant measurement noise of 0.3 log units, and the mean predicted σ (0.54). The standard errors come from the released challenge files, one per compound.

The standard errors are small relative to σ. The median is 0.12 log units (5th to 95th percentile 0.06 to 0.32), against a mean σ of 0.54, and only 2 of 260 exceed the compound's σ. A curve-fit standard error describes how well one curve was fitted within one run, so it probably understates the variation between repeat runs. The standard errors also correlate with the model's σ, with a Spearman correlation of 0.54. Weakly active compounds have flat dose-response curves whose pEC50 is poorly determined (the Spearman correlation between si and pEC50 is −0.83), and the model also gives weakly active compounds a higher σ (the Spearman correlation between σi and pEC50 is −0.53). A good predictive σ should behave this way: some of the error it predicts comes from the assay, and compounds with poorly determined curves are harder to predict.

Where the resampled values center

The resampled values scatter around the measurement, not the true value. However many we draw, they give back the measurement with an error bar of width si, and they tell us nothing new about where the true value ti lies. Resampling does change ρ. Each resampled residual is the observed residual plus a fresh draw of width si, so the compounds with the largest si receive the most added scatter.

What resampling does when the truth is known

To test what the change means, we simulate data in which the true values are known. Each simulated compound has its real predicted σi. Its true value is the prediction plus a model error, and its one measurement is the true value plus measurement noise. The measurement noise has standard deviation si (or a constant 0.3 log units), and the model error receives the rest of σi, a standard deviation of √(σi² − si²), so the simulated model is exactly calibrated, as in the replicate curve of section 04. We then compute ρ between σi and the absolute residual three times, measuring the residual from the prediction to the true value, to the one measurement, and to the resampled measurement (averaged over 20 resamples). The first is the oracle ρ. We repeat this for 1,000 datasets under each noise model.

With a constant noise of 0.3, resampling lowers ρ from 0.28 to 0.22, further from the oracle ρ of 0.42. The added noise is unrelated to σ, so it only scrambles the ranking. With the curve-fit si, resampling raises ρ slightly, from 0.28 to 0.29, further above the oracle ρ of 0.27. That rise is an artifact of the added noise. The noise is largest for compounds with a large si, which tend to have a large σ, so it inflates their residuals and builds in a correlation with σ.

The real data behave as the simulation predicts. Resampling with si gives a mean ρ of 0.28 (95% of resamples between 0.22 and 0.35), above the observed 0.26. Resampling with a constant 0.3 gives 0.22 (0.13 to 0.30), below it. The resampled values are no better than the observed value as estimates of how well σ ranks the compounds, because the noise we chose to add determined the size and direction of the change.

Back to the three readings

  • The measurement is the true value plus noise. This is the model the known-truth simulation uses, and resampling does not move ρ toward the oracle ρ: 0.22 against 0.42 with a constant noise, and 0.29 against 0.27 with si.
  • The measurement is the true value. The observed 0.26 is exact, and resampling moves ρ away from it, to 0.28 with si or 0.22 with a constant noise.
  • The measurement locates the true value to within its standard error. The resampled values in the simulation are exactly these flat-prior draws, so the same comparison applies, and resampled ρ misses the oracle ρ it is meant to estimate. The flat prior ignores that true values cluster around the predictions, so the draws land too far from ŷi, with a variance of σi² + si² around the prediction where the true values have σi² − si². A prior that captured the clustering would have to come from σ itself, which would assume the calibration that ρ is meant to test.

Simulating a separate true value therefore does not assume the answer. Under each reading, resampling fails to sharpen the ranking.

What the curve-fit standard errors are good for

  • They show that σ tracks real assay uncertainty. Compounds the model flags as uncertain tend to have less certain measurements (Spearman correlation 0.54), as a calibrated σ should. Most of the overlap runs through potency: with pEC50 accounted for (a partial rank correlation), the correlation falls to 0.20, so the shared signal is largely that weakly active compounds have flat, poorly determined curves. This part of σ predicts error that a repeat experiment would redraw, not error that more training data could reduce.
  • They put a size on the measurement part of the one-flip problem. With si as the measurement noise, even unlimited replicates would raise ρ only to about 0.36 (section 04). Most of the limit on ρ comes from the model-error draw, which repeat experiments cannot redraw.
  • They let us test whether poorly fitted curves explain the weak ranking. In the better-fitted half of the test set (si at or below the median, 130 compounds), ρ = 0.19, inside the range of 0.04 to 0.36 that a correct σ would produce. In the worse-fitted half, ρ = 0.30, inside 0.15 to 0.46. Each half scores what a correct σ would, so poorly fitted curves do not explain the low ρ.
Takeaway

Resampling a measurement around itself propagates our uncertainty about that measurement but cannot reduce it. One measurement and one prediction per compound limit the ranking information in the test set. Replicates could remove the measurement part of that limit, which the curve-fit standard errors suggest is small, and the model-error part would remain.

08 What this means in practice

The calibration plot (Figure 6 right in the blog post) already told us the intervals are the right width. The σ distribution tells us the model differentiates between compounds. Together, those two facts mean the uncertainty estimates are useful.

What you can do with them: use the predicted intervals directly. If compound A has a 90% interval of [4.5, 6.5] and compound B has [5.0, 5.4], pick B when you need precision. Over a portfolio of decisions, calibrated intervals with real σ variation will outperform ignoring them.

What you can't do: look at one compound's error after the fact and judge whether the model "knew" it was hard. That's the one-flip problem. Repeated measurements would average away the measurement noise, and the model's error on that compound would remain one draw from the distribution σ describes. Across a batch, the ranking is still present.

The reliability diagram in section 01 already showed calibration passing. Several evaluation tools may handle the ranking question better than Spearman ρ on individual residuals, though each comes with its own assumptions and limitations. The first two (quintile binning and CRPS) address compound-level enrichment directly. The third (NLL) is an aggregate model-comparison metric that does not support enrichment.

The one-flip problem limits every evaluation tool that follows. Each method works around it (by aggregating, by scoring the full distribution, by testing calibration globally), but none eliminates it. Replicate measurements would sharpen all of them: more observations per compound would reduce the noise in per-compound error estimates, tighten the quintile bins, widen the gap between per-compound σ and constant σconst in CRPS and NLL, and raise Spearman ρ along the replicate curve in section 04, up to its plateau. The evaluation tools below are the best we can do with one measurement per compound.

08a Quintile binning

The simplest approach: sort the 260 compounds by predicted σ, split them into five equal groups (Q1 to Q5, ~52 each), and compute the mean absolute residual in each bin. Pooling 52 compounds per group averages out the single-draw noise that makes the raw scatter plot so cloudy. If the ranking is real, mean error should increase across the bins.

Quintile binning: mean |residual| by predicted σ group
Mean absolute residual increases from 0.27 in the lowest-σ group (Q1) to 0.65 in the highest (Q5). The ranking is real; pooling compounds per bin reveals the trend that individual residuals obscure.

Strengths: Easy to compute and interpret. Answers the ranking question directly, without modeling assumptions. Weaknesses: The number of bins is arbitrary (5 here, but 4 or 6 would give a slightly different picture). Discretizing throws away information about the shape within each bin, and the approach gives no single comparable score to rank one model's uncertainty against another's.

08b Continuous ranked probability score (CRPS)

CRPS evaluates the full predicted distribution against each observed value. It measures the area between the predicted cumulative distribution function (CDF) F and a step function at the observed value: CRPS = ∫ (Fi(u) − 𝟙[u ≥ yi])² du. A prediction that concentrates probability near the true value scores well; one that spreads probability away from it scores poorly. Lower is better.

CRPS is a proper scoring rule, meaning a model scores best by reporting its true beliefs. It jointly rewards calibration (intervals the right width) and sharpness (intervals as narrow as possible while remaining calibrated). A model that predicts wide intervals for every compound will be calibrated but not sharp; CRPS penalizes the unnecessary width.

The decisive test: does the model's per-compound σ variation help, compared to assigning every compound the same global σconst (the mean of σ, 0.54)?

Strengths: Uses the full predicted distribution, as opposed to a point estimate. Proper scoring rule (a model minimizes CRPS by reporting its true predicted distribution, so there is no incentive to distort the estimate). Works per-observation without binning. Produces a single comparable number across models. Weaknesses: Less intuitive than "did the true value land in the interval?" Requires the full predicted CDF (or a closed-form assumption, as with the Gaussian here). Can be dominated by a few large-error outliers.

08c Negative log-likelihood (NLL)

NLL answers a different question from the first two tools. Where quintile binning and CRPS ask "can the model rank which compounds are hard?", NLL asks "how good is the model's probabilistic prediction in aggregate?" It is a model-comparison metric, not a compound-level triage tool.

Under a Gaussian prediction with mean ŷ and standard deviation σ, NLL = 0.5 × log(2πσi²) + (yi − ŷi)² / (2σi²). The first term penalizes wide intervals (wasted probability); the second penalizes narrow intervals that miss the true value. Lower is better. NLL is a proper scoring rule, and compared to CRPS, it penalizes overconfidence more harshly: a single observation far outside a narrow interval produces a very large NLL, because the squared residual is divided by a small σ².

Per-compound σ beats constant σconst by 3.4%, a slightly larger margin than CRPS, confirming that the variable uncertainty adds value as a whole. Unlike CRPS, NLL per quintile is not monotonic: Q1 scores best (0.32), but Q2 and Q3 score worse than Q4. This happens because NLL penalizes both directions. A moderate-σ compound with one large residual (a bad single draw) gets hammered by the (yi − ŷi)² / σi² term, while a high-σ compound with the same residual absorbs it more gracefully. This non-monotonicity means NLL cannot be used for enrichment: sorting compounds by NLL would not reliably separate easy from hard predictions.

Mean NLL by predicted σ quintile
NLL is not monotonic across quintiles. Q1 benefits from narrow σ and small residuals. Q2 and Q3 are penalized by a few large residuals against moderate σ values. Q5 has the highest NLL because its residuals are genuinely large. The non-monotonicity reflects NLL's sensitivity to tail events, not a calibration failure.

Strengths: Proper scoring rule. The strictest aggregate test of whether σ matches the actual error distribution. Standard in probabilistic modeling; directly optimized by many training procedures. Useful for comparing one model's uncertainty against another's. Weaknesses: Does not support enrichment or compound-level triage (non-monotonic per quintile). Heavily influenced by outliers (a single large residual against a small σ dominates the average). Assumes a distributional form (Gaussian here). Harder to interpret than CRPS or binning in absolute terms.
